# Common errors

These are English errors that a spell-checker lets through, because each wrong form is a real word. They are grouped by kind, and each has the fix and the source of the rule. Most need a human reading; the linter catches only a few of them. Bad examples are in inline code or code blocks so the linter skips them.

## Confused words

Each pair below passes a spell-check either way. Read the sentence for meaning, not for spelling.

### Its and it's

"Its" is the possessive: belonging to it. "It's" is the contraction of "it is" or "it has." Source: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide, possessive its](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/i/its-vs-its), checked 2026-09-27.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `The app saves it's settings.` | The app saves its settings. |
| `Its ready when the light turns green.` | It's ready when the light turns green. |

Test: replace the word with "it is." If the sentence still works, write "it's."

### Affect and effect

"Affect" is a verb meaning to influence or change something. "Effect" is a noun meaning the result of an influence. Source: [Cambridge Dictionary: affect or effect](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/affect-or-effect), checked 2026-09-27.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `The update won't effect your saved files.` | The update won't affect your saved files. |
| `You'll notice the affect in a week.` | You'll notice the effect in a week. |

"Effect" is also a formal verb meaning to make something happen, as in "effect a change." Source: [Cambridge Dictionary, effect](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/effect), checked 2026-09-27. In copy, "make" or "bring about" is plainer.

### Fewer and less

Use "fewer" for things you can count and "less" for an amount, value, or degree. Do not use "under" for a quantity. Source: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide, less vs. fewer vs. under](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/l/less-vs-fewer-vs-under), checked 2026-09-27.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `Less steps to check out.` | Fewer steps to check out. |
| `Orders under 20 items ship free.` | Orders of fewer than 20 items ship free. |
| `Fewer sugar than before.` | Less sugar than before. |

A price or a weight is an amount: "less than $50," "less than 2 kg."

### Comprise

The whole comprises its parts: "The kit comprises a grinder, a filter, and a scale." Cambridge also accepts the passive form `is comprised of`. Source: [Cambridge Dictionary on comprise](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/comprise), checked 2026-09-27.

"Consists of," "includes," or "has" are plainer. Keep "includes" for a partial list, because it tells the reader that more items exist.

### Who and that

Use "who" for people and "that" or "which" for things. Source: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide, who vs. that](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/w/who-vs-that), checked 2026-09-27.

Microsoft also separates "that" and "which." Use "that," with no comma, for a clause the sentence needs. Use "which," after a comma, for a clause you could delete. Source: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide, that vs. which](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/t/that-vs-which), checked 2026-09-27.

| Microsoft avoids | Microsoft writes |
|---|---|
| `Customers that order before noon get same-day delivery.` | Customers who order before noon get same-day delivery. |
| `The plan, that includes support, costs $20.` | The plan, which includes support, costs $20. |

These are Microsoft's house rules, not errors in every variant. In a clause the sentence needs, "who" and "that" can both refer to people, and "that" and "which" are both common for things. Many American style guides keep "which" for the optional clause; that is a style preference, not a rule of grammar. Source: [Wikipedia: English relative clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_relative_clauses), revision 1359461429, checked 2026-09-28. In `en-GB` copy, follow the brand's own habit. One point holds in every variant: a clause set off by a comma takes "which" or "who," never "that."

## Grammar

These structures are grammatical enough to pass a quick read but point the reader at the wrong thing.

### Dangling modifiers

A dangling modifier modifies nothing in the sentence. A misplaced modifier sits too far from what it describes, or too close to something else. Short sentences in the active voice rarely have either problem. Source: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide: dangling and misplaced modifiers](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/grammar/dangling-misplaced-modifiers), checked 2026-09-27.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `After baking for an hour, you can slice the loaf.` | After an hour in the oven, the loaf is ready to slice. |
| `Designed for small teams, you'll set it up in minutes.` | The app is designed for small teams, and setup takes [confirm: minutes] minutes. |
| `We only ship to Canada on weekdays.` | We ship to Canada only on weekdays. |

Test: the phrase at the start of a sentence must describe the subject that comes right after the comma.

### Collective nouns in US and UK English

British English lets a collective noun take a singular or a plural verb. The choice depends on whether the group acts as one body or as individuals: "the committee was appointed," "the committee were unable to agree." American English almost always uses the singular: "the committee was unable to agree." Source: [Wikipedia: American and British English grammatical differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_and_British_English_grammatical_differences), revision 1370565230, checked 2026-09-27. Cambridge lists audience, crew, and public among the nouns that can take either form. Source: [Cambridge Dictionary on singular and plural nouns](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/nouns-singular-and-plural), checked 2026-09-27.

For copy:

- Follow the variant. In `en-US`, a company or a team is singular, as in "the team is on call until 10 p.m."
- Keep the verb and the pronoun in step. "The team is ... its" or, in British English, "the team are ... their." Never switch inside one text.
- Brand names follow the same rule. Decide once, record the choice in the voice profile, and keep it across channels.

## Punctuation

These marks change meaning when they move.

### Apostrophes

Source for this section: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide, apostrophes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/punctuation/apostrophes), checked 2026-09-27.

- A singular noun takes an apostrophe and "s," even when it ends in s, x, or z: "the bus's route," "the box's contents." This is Microsoft's rule. The Associated Press Stylebook drops the extra s after a proper noun that ends in s. It keeps it after one that ends in z or x. Source: [Wikipedia: Apostrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostrophe), revision 1376805518, checked 2026-09-28. Follow the house style the profile sets.
- A plural noun ending in s takes only the apostrophe: "users' passwords."
- Contractions use an apostrophe for the missing letters: "can't," "it's."
- No apostrophe in "its," in possessive pronouns such as "yours," or in plain plurals.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `New flavor's every week.` | New flavors every week. |
| `The team's are ready.` | The teams are ready. |
| `Your order is your's to keep.` | Your order is yours to keep. |

Abbreviations form the plural like other words, with no apostrophe: "APIs," not `API's`. Source: [Google developer documentation style guide, abbreviations](https://developers.google.com/style/abbreviations), checked 2026-09-27.

### Hyphens in compound adjectives

Source for this section: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide, hyphens](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/punctuation/dashes-hyphens/hyphens), checked 2026-09-27.

- Hyphenate two words that work as one modifier before a noun when the reader could misread them without the hyphen: "up-to-date information," "built-in drive."
- Hyphenate when one word is a participle or when the modifier starts with a number: "well-defined plan," "two-hour class."
- Do not hyphenate after an adverb ending in -ly, or after "very": "highly rated app," "very fast delivery."
- After the noun, the hyphen usually goes: "The information is up to date." Microsoft's own example is "The schema is well defined."

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `a fully-refundable ticket` | a fully refundable ticket |
| `a 30 day free trial` | a 30-day free trial |
| `Our prices are up-to-date.` | Our prices are up to date. |

## One word or two

Many English phrasal verbs are two words, while the noun or adjective made from them is one. Pick the form by its job in the sentence.

### Log in and login

"Log in" is a phrasal verb, and "login" (also `log-in`) is a noun. Sources: [Cambridge Dictionary, log in](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/log-in) and [Cambridge Dictionary, login](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/login), both checked 2026-09-27.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `Login to see your orders.` | Log in to see your orders. |
| `Forgot your log in details?` | Forgot your login details? |

Microsoft goes further: it writes "sign in" and "sign out," and uses `log in` only when the interface says so. It also avoids "sign-in" as a noun or adjective. Source: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide, sign in, sign out](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/s/sign-in-sign-out), checked 2026-09-27.

For a product, choose "sign in" or "log in" once and use it on every screen, email, and help page. Put the unused form in the profile's `avoid` list with the chosen one in `prefer`, so the linter flags it (`en-profile-avoid`).

### Set up and setup

"Set up" is two words as a verb, and "setup" is one word as a noun or adjective, with no hyphen. Source: [Microsoft Writing Style Guide, set up, setup](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/s/set-up-setup), checked 2026-09-27.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `Setup your account in two steps.` | Set up your account in two steps. |
| `The set up takes five minutes.` | The setup takes five minutes. |

Cambridge lists the noun as `set-up` in its British entry and as "setup" in its American one. Sources: [Cambridge Dictionary, set-up](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/set-up) and [Cambridge Dictionary, setup](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/setup), both checked 2026-09-28. In `en-GB` copy, `set-up` follows the British entry. Whichever form you choose, keep it across the product.

Look up any other pair of this kind in a dictionary before it goes into a product.

### Every day and everyday

"Every day" is two words when it means each day. "Everyday" is one word, an adjective meaning normal or usual. Source: [Cambridge Dictionary, every](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/every), checked 2026-09-27.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `We bake fresh everyday.` | We bake fresh every day. |
| `Bread for every day meals.` | Bread for everyday meals. |

Test: if "single" fits between the words, as in "every single day," write two words.

## Checklist

The linter does not check most of these pairs; they need a human reading. Rule ids that help: `en-profile-avoid` and `en-profile-banned` for glossary terms such as "log in" or "sign in," and `en-spelling-mix` for variant spelling.

- [ ] "Its" and "it's" pass the "it is" test.
- [ ] "Affect" is the verb and "effect" the noun, unless "effect" means to bring about.
- [ ] "Fewer" goes with things you count, "less" with amounts, and "under" never means "fewer than."
- [ ] Who, that, and which follow the house style; Microsoft style uses "who" for people, "that" for a needed clause, and "which" after a comma.
- [ ] The phrase at the start of each sentence describes the subject after the comma.
- [ ] Collective nouns take the variant's verb form, and the pronouns agree.
- [ ] No apostrophe in plurals or in "its."
- [ ] Compound modifiers before a noun are hyphenated; none follow an -ly adverb.
- [ ] Verbs are two words ("log in," "set up") and nouns one ("login," "setup").
- [ ] One sign-in term is used across the whole product.
