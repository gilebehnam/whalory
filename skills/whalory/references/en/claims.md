# Sensitive claims

Some claims need evidence before English copy can make them: superlatives, studies, reviews, prices, health, the environment, origin, money, and AI. This file says what each claim needs and what Whalory writes when the evidence is missing. It covers the United States (US), the United Kingdom (UK), and the European Union (EU) as far as the research for this pack went. Other markets get the risk layer and "[local rules to be confirmed]". The rules here guide the writer. Final legal calls belong to the business and its counsel.

## Evidence before claim

A claim is only as strong as the evidence the brand holds on the day it publishes. Two rules state this directly:

- In the US, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) requires a reasonable basis for an objective claim before the ad runs. A claim that names a level of support, such as `tests prove`, needs at least that support. Source: [FTC Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation), checked 2026-09-27.
- In the UK, the Committee of Advertising Practice (CAP) Code applies. Its rule 3.7 requires documentary evidence, held before publication, for claims a reader would take as objective. Rule 3.1 bans materially misleading ads. Source: [CAP Code, section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html), checked 2026-09-27.

What Whalory does:

1. List every objective claim in the draft: numbers, rankings, results, origins, awards, and comparisons.
2. For each, ask whether the brief or a source the brand can show supports it, in these exact words.
3. Keep supported claims as they are. Replace unsupported ones with a bracket, or rewrite them as something the brand can show.
4. Name every bracket and every claim that needs sign-off in the note.

## Puffery and measurable superlatives

Obvious exaggeration that no reader takes literally is puffery, and the CAP Code allows it when it does not materially mislead (rule 3.2). The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) treats an unqualified superlative as a comparison with every competing product. Superiority claims need evidence unless they are obvious puffery, and an objective superiority claim must say which aspect is better. Source: [CAP Code, section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html), checked 2026-09-27.

| Claim | What it needs |
|---|---|
| `the best`, `the leading`, `#1` | A measure, a market, a date, and a source: "the top-selling [confirm: product] in [confirm: market], [source needed: sales data and period]" |
| "the first," "the only" | Proof that no one did it before, or that no one else does it now, with the scope stated |
| "faster," "cheaper," "stronger" | What it is compared with, how it was measured, and when |
| "the tastiest coffee in town" | Obvious puffery, usually fine, but it adds nothing; a fact does more |

The rule id is `en-superlative`, a warning. A claim that cannot carry a source becomes a plain fact about the product instead.

## Common sales claims

| Claim | Evidence or wording it needs | Source (checked 2026-09-27) |
|---|---|---|
| `guaranteed`, `guarantee` | Must not confuse the reader about their legal rights; significant limits stated; full terms before the reader commits | [CAP 3.55 and 3.56](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) |
| `up to 50% off`, `from $9` | Must not exaggerate how many products or how much benefit is really available | [CAP 3.22](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) |
| "approved by," "certified," a trust mark | The approval or authorization must exist, and its terms must be followed | [CAP 3.52](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) |
| A legal right shown as a perk, such as a statutory refund right | Banned as a distinctive feature | [CAP 3.12](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) and [Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers (DMCC) Act 2024, Schedule 20, paragraph 11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20) |
| "only today," "last few left" | Must be true; see [fake urgency](ethics.md#fake-urgency-and-scarcity) | [CAP 3.30](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) |
| "award-winning" | The award's name, year, and awarding body, held as evidence | [CAP 3.7](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) |
| "handmade," "natural," "original," "local" | Documentary evidence that the word is literally true for this product | [CAP 3.7](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) |

In financial copy, `guaranteed` has its own limits; see [financial promotions](#financial-promotions).

## Establishment claims

An establishment claim says the proof already exists: `clinically proven`, `studies show`, `tests confirm`, `doctors recommend`. The FTC policy statement above requires at least the level of support the claim states. The rule id is `en-establishment-claim`, an error.

Before such a claim stays, the brand supplies:

- who ran the study, and whether they were independent;
- when it ran, how many people or samples it had, and what it measured;
- the result, in the study's words;
- whether the product in the study is the product being sold.

| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| `Clinically proven to reduce wrinkles.` | "In a [confirm: study length] study of [confirm: sample size] people run by [confirm: lab], [confirm: result in the study's words]." |
| `Doctors recommend our vitamins.` | [source needed: survey of doctors, sample size, question asked, date] |

When the brand cannot supply the study, rewrite the line as a fact about the product itself.

## Testimonials and reviews

Reviews and testimonials are real, verbatim, and consented, or they do not appear.

United States:

- The FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials took effect on October 21, 2024. Its citation in the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) is 16 CFR Part 465. The rule bans fake reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones, and reviews from people who do not exist. It also bans incentives tied to positive or negative sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, and company-run "independent" review sites. Suppressing reviews and buying fake followers or views are banned too. Sources: [16 CFR Part 465 on eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465) and [FTC announcement of the final rule](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials), checked 2026-09-27.
- The FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255, require endorsements to reflect honest opinions and material connections to be disclosed clearly. When the results shown are not typical, the ad must show the generally expected results. Source: [16 CFR Part 255 on eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255), checked 2026-09-27.

United Kingdom:

- CAP rules 3.44 to 3.46 ban fake consumer reviews and require incentivized reviews to be made clear. They also ban publishing reviews in a misleading way. Hiding negative reviews while showing positive ones is one example. Rules 3.47 to 3.50 require documentary evidence that a testimonial is genuine, contact details for its author, relevance to the product, and permission. Source: [CAP Code, section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html), checked 2026-09-27.
- The DMCC Act 2024 bans submitting or commissioning fake reviews and concealing that a review was incentivized. Source: [DMCC Act 2024, Schedule 20, paragraph 13](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20), checked 2026-09-27.

European Union:

- The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive bans claiming that reviews come from real users without reasonable checks. It also bans fake or commissioned reviews (Annex I, points 23b and 23c). Source: [European Commission, Unfair Commercial Practices Directive](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/consumer-protection-law/unfair-commercial-practices-and-price-indication/unfair-commercial-practices-directive_en), checked 2026-09-27.

What Whalory does:

- Uses only quotes the brand supplies, word for word, with the customer's consent. Trimming for length is allowed only with an ellipsis and without changing the meaning.
- Never writes a review, a quote, or a star rating, and never "improves" one.
- Labels incentivized reviews as such, and states typical results next to standout ones.
- Writes `[testimonial needed]` where the draft wants a quote the brief does not have. The linter flags a quotation followed by a name (`en-testimonial`).

## Real people in copy

Names, photos, stories, and quotes of real people appear only with their permission. The CAP Code urges written permission before an ad portrays a member of the public or a person with a public profile. The same applies before it implies their approval. Source: [CAP Code, section 6](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/06.html), checked 2026-09-27.

- Never present a composite or invented person as real. A persona used for planning stays in the planning document.
- A customer story needs the customer's consent to the exact final text, not just to "being featured."
- Staff profiles follow the same rule: no age, history, or quote without the person's sign-off.

## Price claims

Price copy is where small words carry legal weight. In the UK, an ad that quotes a price must state the total price, including any fees, taxes, or charges the consumer will necessarily pay. Sources: [CAP rule 3.4.3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) and [DMCC Act 2024, section 230](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/section/230), checked 2026-09-27. Fees that appear only later in checkout are drip pricing ([ethics.md](ethics.md#drip-pricing)).

### Reference prices

United States:

- The FTC Guides Against Deceptive Pricing accept a former price for a "was" claim only when it is the actual, bona fide price. The product must have been offered to the public at that price on a regular basis, for a reasonably substantial period. A price raised for a few days so that it can be "cut" makes the bargain false (16 CFR 233.1(a) and (c)).
- A former price does not need sales at that price, but it must have been openly and actively offered in good faith. Words such as "formerly sold at" imply real sales, so they need substantial sales at that price (16 CFR 233.1(b)).

Source: [16 CFR Part 233 on eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233), checked 2026-09-28.

United Kingdom:

- A price comparison must make its basis clear (CAP 3.38).
- A comparison with a recommended retail price (RRP) is likely to mislead if the product generally sells at a very different price (CAP 3.39).

Source: [CAP Code, section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html), checked 2026-09-27.

| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| `Was $80, now $49!` | "Now $49. Our price from [confirm: dates] was $80." |
| `Formerly sold at $80` | "Our regular price from [confirm: dates] was $80." Keep "sold at" only when the brand confirms substantial sales at $80 |
| `RRP $120, our price $60` | [source needed: evidence the product generally sells at $120] |

### The 30-day prior price

In the EU, any announced price reduction must show the prior price (Price Indication Directive, Article 6a). That is the trader's lowest price in at least the 30 days before the reduction. The Court of Justice ruled on September 26, 2024, in case C-330/23 that the percentage off is calculated on that prior price. Member states may set exceptions, for example for perishable goods. Source: [European Commission, Price Indication Directive](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/consumer-protection-law/unfair-commercial-practices-and-price-indication/price-indication-directive_en), checked 2026-09-27.

For EU copy, ask for the lowest price of the last 30 days before writing "was" or `-30%`. Without it, write `[confirm: lowest price in the 30 days before the sale]`.

### Free offers

In the US, the FTC's guide on the word "free" sets the rule. When an item is "free" with another item, the reader pays nothing for it. They also pay no more than the regular price for the item they buy. The regular price is the price the product openly and actively sold at for a reasonably substantial period, which the guide puts at 30 days. Every term, condition, and obligation of the offer appears clearly and at the outset, close to the word "free." A footnote reached through an asterisk does not count. Calling the item a "gift" or a "bonus" does not rescue an offer that fails these tests. Source: [16 CFR Part 251 on eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-251), checked 2026-09-28.

In the UK, "free" means the reader pays nothing beyond the unavoidable cost of responding and of delivery. Packing or handling charges, a raised price on the item they must buy, or a lower-quality item all break the rule. The ad must also make clear what the reader must commit to. "Free trial" cannot describe a money-back offer, or an offer where the reader must buy something they cannot get refunded. Sources: [CAP rules 3.23 to 3.26](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html) and [DMCC Act 2024, Schedule 20, paragraph 23](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20), checked 2026-09-27.

A free trial that turns into a paid subscription is a negative option offer in the US. Online, the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA) requires three things. The seller discloses all material terms clearly and conspicuously before taking billing information. It gets the reader's express informed consent before charging. It provides a simple way to stop recurring charges. Source: [US Code, title 15, section 8403, on the Legal Information Institute site](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403), checked 2026-09-28.

- The FTC's 2024 amendments to its Negative Option Rule, known as the "click-to-cancel" rule, are not in force. After federal court decisions, the FTC restored the earlier text of the rule on February 12, 2026. On March 13, 2026, it asked for public comment on whether the rule needs amending. Sources: [Federal Register, February 12, 2026](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/02/12/2026-02866/revision-of-the-negative-option-rule-withdrawal-of-the-cars-rule-removal-of-the-non-compete-rule-to) and [Federal Register, March 13, 2026](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/13/2026-04952/rule-concerning-the-use-of-prenotification-negative-option-plans), checked 2026-09-28.
- A state can add its own automatic-renewal law. California's, for example, requires the renewal terms clearly and conspicuously before the agreement and next to the request for consent. A free trial must also explain the price charged after it ends, and the business needs the consumer's express affirmative consent. Source: [California Business and Professions Code, section 17602](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=17602), checked 2026-09-28. This pack has not checked other states' automatic-renewal laws, so copy for readers in other states carries `[local rules to be confirmed]`.

For copy, put three facts next to the button that starts the trial: the price after the trial, the renewal period, and how to cancel. "Free for [confirm: trial length], then [confirm: price] every [confirm: period] until you cancel. Cancel any time in [confirm: where]." Dark-pattern versions of this offer are in [ethics.md](ethics.md#confirmshaming-and-other-dark-patterns).

## Health claims

- In the US, health claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence, which for health benefits generally means randomized controlled trials in people. Adding "may help" does not rescue an unsupported claim. Source: [FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance), checked 2026-09-27.
- A dietary supplement may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent a disease. A structure or function claim needs this disclaimer, word for word:

  ```
  This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.
  ```

  Source: [US Food and Drug Administration (FDA): structure and function claims under 21 CFR 101.93](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/structurefunction-claims), checked 2026-09-27. The manufacturer also notifies the FDA of the claim; the writer asks whether that was done.
- Before and after photos, cure stories, and "doctor-approved" need the evidence of an [establishment claim](#establishment-claims).
- Health rules for other markets are not covered in this pack. Write "[local rules to be confirmed]" and flag the claim as high risk.

## Green claims

The EU green transition directive for consumers, (EU) 2024/825, applies from September 27, 2026. It bans:

- generic environmental claims such as "eco-friendly," "green," or "climate-friendly" without recognized excellent environmental performance;
- sustainability labels that are not based on a certification scheme or set up by a public authority;
- a claim about the whole product when only one part of it qualifies;
- claims that a product has a neutral or reduced climate impact because of emissions offsetting;
- presenting a legal requirement as a distinctive feature.

A claim about future performance, such as `net zero by 2030`, needs a detailed and realistic plan. The plan has time-bound targets and is checked by an independent third party. Source: [European Commission, sustainable consumption](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/consumers/consumer-rights-and-complaints/sustainable-consumption_en), checked 2026-09-27.

For copy: say the specific, provable fact. "Box made from [confirm: percentage]% recycled cardboard, [source needed: supplier certificate]" instead of `eco-friendly packaging`. Green-claim rules for the US and the UK are not covered in this pack; write "[local rules to be confirmed]".

## Made in USA claims

An unqualified "Made in USA" claim is allowed only when final assembly and all significant processing happen in the US. All or virtually all components must also be made and sourced there. Otherwise the claim is qualified, as in "Assembled in USA from imported parts." The rule covers labels, and seals or marks in mail-order and online promotion. Source: [FTC Made in USA Labeling Rule, 16 CFR Part 323](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-323), checked 2026-09-27.

Origin claims for other countries follow the same habit: ask where each step happens, and bracket what the brief does not say.

## Financial promotions

- US investment advisers follow the Marketing Rule of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), 17 CFR 275.206(4)-1. It bans untrue or unsubstantiated statements and misleading implications, and requires fair and balanced treatment of risks. Testimonials need disclosures about client status, pay, and conflicts. Performance is shown net of fees, with gross performance no more prominent. Source: [17 CFR 275.206(4)-1 on eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-II/part-275/section-275.206(4)-1), checked 2026-09-27.
- In the UK, the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) sets the rules in its Conduct of Business Sourcebook (COBS). Under COBS 4.2, a financial promotion must be fair, clear, and not misleading. It must say plainly when capital is at risk. Only authorized or approved persons may communicate one, under section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA). Under guidance FG24/1, each social media post must be compliant on its own, with the required risk warning on its face. Source: [Financial Conduct Authority handbook, COBS 4.2](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/cobs4/cobs4s2), checked 2026-09-27.

For copy: never promise a return or call one `guaranteed` unless the firm confirms it is accurate and explains it in full. Leave the risk warning as a bracket for the firm to supply: `[confirm: required risk warning]`. Ask who approves the promotion before it goes out.

## AI disclosure

- Article 50 of the EU AI Act applies from August 2, 2026. People must be told when they are dealing with an AI system unless it is obvious. Deepfakes must be disclosed. AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be disclosed, unless a person reviewed it and holds editorial responsibility. Source: [AI Act, Article 50 overview](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/), checked 2026-09-27.
- In the US, 16 CFR Part 465 bans AI-generated fake reviews and testimonials ([testimonials and reviews](#testimonials-and-reviews)).
- A claim about an AI feature is an objective claim. `Our AI answers 90% of questions` needs the same evidence as any other statistic.

## Discriminatory language

- A job ad must not state or suggest a preference that screens people out by a protected trait. In the US, an employer may not publish a job ad that shows a preference based on a protected trait. The traits include race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age (40 or older), disability, and genetic information. The US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) gives "recent college graduates" as a phrase that may discourage older applicants. Source: [EEOC: prohibited employment policies and practices](https://www.eeoc.gov/prohibited-employment-policiespractices), checked 2026-09-27.
- In the UK, ads must not cause serious or widespread offense. The CAP Code asks for particular care on grounds such as age, disability, gender, race, religion, sex, and sexual orientation. Source: [CAP Code, section 4, rule 4.1](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/04.html), checked 2026-09-27.
- For copy: describe the job and the skills it needs, not the person. "Two years of bookkeeping experience" instead of `young and energetic`. No jokes at a group's expense.

## Bracket markers

When the evidence is missing, the copy keeps a visible bracket instead of a guess.

| Marker | Use it for |
|---|---|
| `[confirm: …]` | Facts the brand knows but the brief does not give: a weight, a date, a price, a name |
| `[source needed: …]` | Numbers, studies, rankings, or comparisons that need a source before they can stay |
| `[testimonial needed]` | Where the draft wants a real customer's words |
| `[local rules to be confirmed]` | Markets this pack does not cover |

The linter does not treat these as template residue; it counts them under `stats.placeholders`. The note lists each bracket, and each claim that needs sign-off, after the diagnosis line ([router.md](../router.md#diagnosis-note)). In an automated run, they go under `needs_verification` in the JSON output ([router.md](../router.md#delivery-by-host-and-intent)).

## Checklist

Linter rule ids: `en-superlative`, `en-establishment-claim`, `en-stat-claim`, `en-vague-attribution`, `en-testimonial`, `en-fact-not-in-brief` (with `--facts`), `en-template-residue`.

- [ ] Every objective claim traces to evidence the brand holds today, or has a bracket.
- [ ] Superlatives name their measure, market, and date, or are cut.
- [ ] No `clinically proven`, `studies show`, or `doctors recommend` without the study itself.
- [ ] Reviews and quotes are real, verbatim, and consented; incentives are labeled.
- [ ] Real people appear only with their permission.
- [ ] Prices show the total; "was" prices and RRPs have a basis; EU reductions use the 30-day lowest price.
- [ ] A US "was" price was really offered for a reasonably substantial period.
- [ ] "Free" costs the reader nothing beyond responding and delivery, and its conditions sit next to the word.
- [ ] A trial that renews states the later price, the period, and how to cancel, next to the button.
- [ ] No disease claim for a supplement; the FDA disclaimer is word for word.
- [ ] No generic green claim in EU copy.
- [ ] "Made in USA" meets the all-or-virtually-all test, or is qualified.
- [ ] Financial copy carries its risk warning, and the approver is known.
- [ ] Job ads describe the work, not the person.
- [ ] The note lists every bracket and every claim that needs sign-off.
