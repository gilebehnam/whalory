# Professional ethics

Some lines hold for every brand and every client, whatever the voice profile says. This file covers them for English copy: honesty, pressure, vulnerable readers, disclosure, privacy, and when Whalory declines. It names the US, UK, and EU rules behind each line where the research for this pack covered them. The rules are the floor; the tests below usually stop a text well before a rule does. Final legal calls belong to the business and its counsel.

## Three quick tests

Before delivery, every text passes three questions. A "no" to any of them sends the text back.

| Test | Question | A text that fails |
|---|---|---|
| Face to face | Could you say this sentence to this person, in the shop or the clinic, looking at them? | A "tonight only" text message sent every night |
| Behind the scenes | If the reader saw how this text was made, would they still be fine with it? | A "customer review" written by the sales team |
| A year later | If this text were read again in a year with the writer's name on it, would the writer stand by it? | `Guaranteed 12% returns` for an investment fund |

These three questions are the method's one ethics test. Wherever Whalory mentions an ethics check, from [review.md](../review.md#ethics) to [editor.md](../editor.md#scoring-rubric), it means this table.

Some topics have a one-line test of their own. Run it when the text touches the topic:

| Topic | One-line test |
|---|---|
| Urgency and scarcity | Can you write the reason in one true sentence? ([fake urgency](#fake-urgency-and-scarcity)) |
| Vulnerable readers | If this reaches someone on their worst day, is it information or pressure? ([vulnerable readers](#vulnerable-readers)) |
| News and tragedy | Take the brand name out. Does the message still help anyone? ([occasions.md](occasions.md#grief-and-crisis)) |
| Data | If this input leaked, could anyone be identified? ([consent and privacy](#consent-and-privacy)) |

In an automated run, the result goes in the note: "Ethics: face to face, behind the scenes, a year later; all passed". If one fails, the note names the test and the reason.

## Honesty: claims match the evidence

A text can mislead without a single false sentence. Three habits do it most often:

- Half-truths. The UK Committee of Advertising Practice (CAP) Code bans leaving out material information (rule 3.3). Giving it where the reader is unlikely to notice it counts as leaving it out. It also bans leaving out significant limitations (rule 3.9). Source: [CAP Code, section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html), checked 2026-09-27.
- Numbers without context. `Save 40%` needs the price it is taken off and the period. "Over 1,000 customers" needs a date and a source.
- Promises without conditions. "Free delivery" with a minimum order must state the minimum next to the offer, not in a footnote.

The evidence each claim needs is in [claims.md](claims.md). The rule for the writer: say less than the evidence allows, never more.

## Fake urgency and scarcity

A deadline or a stock level appears only when it is true and the reason fits in one sentence.

- In the UK, it is banned to claim falsely that an offer is available only for a limited time, to force a quick decision. Sources: [Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers (DMCC) Act 2024, Schedule 20, paragraph 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20) and [CAP rule 3.30](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html), both checked 2026-09-27.
- The Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) gives the example of a countdown clock that restarts when it runs out, with the offer still running. Source: [CMA207, unfair commercial practices guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices), updated November 18, 2025, checked 2026-09-27.
- The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) staff report on dark patterns lists baseless countdown timers, false low-stock messages, and false high-demand messages as pressure tactics. Source: [FTC, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, September 2022](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light), checked 2026-09-27.

| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| `Only 2 left!` (no stock feed) | "[confirm: live stock count] left in your size." |
| `Offer ends in 00:59:48` (resets every hour) | "The spring price ends on [confirm: end date and time zone]." |
| `12 people are looking at this right now` | Leave it out unless the number comes from live data. |

## Drip pricing

Showing a headline price and adding mandatory charges later in checkout is drip pricing, and in the UK it is prohibited. An offer that invites the reader to buy must state the total price, including fees, taxes, and charges the consumer will necessarily pay. Sources: [DMCC Act 2024, section 230](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/section/230), [CMA207](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices), and [CAP rule 3.4](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html), all checked 2026-09-27.

For copy:

- The first price the reader sees includes every fee they cannot avoid: booking fees, service charges, and cleaning fees.
- Optional extras are labeled as optional, with their price.
- When part of the price depends on the order, say how it is worked out, as clearly as the price itself.

Price claims in general: [claims.md](claims.md#price-claims).

## Confirmshaming and other dark patterns

A dark pattern is an interface that steers people into a choice they would not make freely. The writer owns the words in that interface, so the writer shares the responsibility.

The FTC staff report defines several patterns that live in copy. Source: [FTC, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light), checked 2026-09-27.

| Pattern | What the report describes | Whalory's version |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmshaming | Shame that frames the other choice as a bad decision, as in `No, I don't want to save money` | "No thanks" |
| Preselection | A default that suits the company, not the user, such as add-on insurance already in the cart | Every add-on starts unticked, with its price |
| Sneak into basket | Items added without permission, or through a pre-checked box | Nothing enters the cart unless the reader adds it |
| Hidden subscription | A one-off order that turns into a recurring charge | "This is a subscription: [confirm: price] every [confirm: period]. Cancel any time in Settings." |
| Hard to cancel | A cancel button that leads somewhere else, or a pitch in the way | "Cancel subscription" cancels. One offer at most, with a clear "Cancel anyway." |

US law adds three duties for online subscriptions: material terms before billing, express informed consent, and a simple way to stop the charges ([free offers](claims.md#free-offers)).

In the EU, consent to marketing must be freely given and shown by a clear affirmative act. Recital 32 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) says that silence, pre-ticked boxes, or inactivity do not count as consent. The Court of Justice applied the same standard to cookies in Planet49 (case C-673/17, October 1, 2019): a pre-ticked checkbox is not valid consent. Sources: [GDPR on EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng) and [the Planet49 judgment on EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62017CJ0673), checked 2026-09-28.

The review checklist has a dark-patterns section of its own: [review.md](../review.md#dark-patterns). Interface wording in depth: ux-strings.md (in Whalory Pro).

## Vulnerable readers

Some readers are more easily harmed by pressure: people in debt, ill, grieving, or very young, and anyone in a crisis.

- UK consumer law judges a practice by the average member of a vulnerable group when the trader could foresee that vulnerability. People can be vulnerable because of age, physical or mental health, credulity, or their circumstances. Source: [CMA207](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices), checked 2026-09-27.
- The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) defines a vulnerable customer as someone especially susceptible to harm because of their personal circumstances. It names poor health, life events, low resilience, and low capability as drivers. Source: [FCA, fair treatment of vulnerable customers (FG21/1)](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/finalised-guidance/guidance-firms-fair-treatment-vulnerable-customers), checked 2026-09-27.
- Ads for children must not exploit their credulity, loyalty, vulnerability, or lack of experience (CAP rule 5.2). Nor may they make a child feel inferior for not buying. They must not appeal to children directly to buy, or to pester adults to buy for them (CAP rule 5.4.2). Sources: [CAP Code, section 5](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/05.html) and [DMCC Act 2024, Schedule 20, paragraph 30](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20), checked 2026-09-27.

For copy:

- Debt, illness, and grief get information and a way out, never a deadline. "If you're having trouble paying, call [confirm: support line]. We can pause payments for [confirm: period]."
- No "act now" in any message to a reader in arrears or in a health setting.
- A message about a death or a diagnosis carries no upsell.
- Child audiences get a clear line between story and product, and no "ask your parents."

## Fear and shame

Ads must not cause fear or distress without a good reason, and when there is one, the fear must not be excessive. A claim or image meant only to shock people into paying attention is banned. Source: [CAP Code, section 4, rule 4.2](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/04.html), checked 2026-09-27.

- No selling through body shame, social shame, or fear of missing out on a life event.
- Safety messages may use real risk, stated in proportion and with the action that reduces it.
- Humor that punches down at a group is out; see [claims.md](claims.md#discriminatory-language).

| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| `Still hiding your arms this summer?` | "Sleeveless linen, in sizes [confirm: size range]." |
| `Don't be the only parent who forgot.` | "Orders placed by [confirm: cut-off date] arrive before [confirm: event date]." |

## Respect for the reader

The reader is an adult with limited time. Five habits waste that time or talk down to them:

- explaining the obvious;
- hidden condescension, such as `As you probably don't know`;
- repeating the pitch in every paragraph;
- a fake personal touch, such as `picked just for you` on a message sent to everyone;
- a sales pitch dressed as a guide or a news story.

The last one has rules behind it. In the UK it is banned to pay for editorial content that promotes a product without saying it was paid for. Source: [DMCC Act 2024, Schedule 20, paragraph 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20), checked 2026-09-27. Mark sponsored guides as ads; see [ad labels](#ad-labels).

## Get-rich-quick and multi-level marketing

Whalory does not write income promises, recruitment-first offers, or promises of fixed returns.

- The FTC's guidance on multi-level marketing (MLM) says an earnings claim must reflect what the typical participant is likely to earn, after expenses. Images of houses, cars, and luxury trips are implied earnings claims. A "results not typical" line does not fix an atypical claim. Source: [FTC, business guidance concerning multi-level marketing](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/business-guidance-concerning-multi-level-marketing), checked 2026-09-27.
- In the UK, running or promoting a pyramid promotional scheme is banned. That is a scheme where people pay for a chance to earn mainly from recruiting others rather than from selling products. Source: [DMCC Act 2024, Schedule 20, paragraph 16](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20), checked 2026-09-27.
- Investment copy follows the financial promotion rules in [claims.md](claims.md#financial-promotions).

What Whalory can write instead is an honest description of the product and of what a participant must buy and spend. Typical results go in only if the company has them. If it does not, the copy makes no earnings claim at all.

## Fake reviews and followers

- Whalory never writes a review, a rating, or a testimonial on anyone's behalf, and never "improves" a real one.
- Social proof numbers come from the platform's own data, with a date: "[confirm: follower count] followers on [confirm: date]".
- Buying or selling fake followers, views, or likes is banned in the US under section 465.8 of the FTC's review rule. Source: [Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), 16 CFR Part 465](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465), checked 2026-09-27.
- The UK and EU rules on fake reviews are in [claims.md](claims.md#testimonials-and-reviews).

## Ad labels

Paid content says it is paid, up front, in words the reader cannot miss.

| Market | What the label must do | Source (checked 2026-09-27) |
|---|---|---|
| UK | An upfront, prominent "Ad" label in each post. A mention in the bio is not enough, and `#aff` alone is not enough. | [Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) and CMA influencers' guide](https://www.asa.org.uk/resource/influencers-guide.html) |
| US | Material connections disclosed clearly and conspicuously. "Ad," "sponsored," and "Advertisement" work; `sp`, `spon`, and `collab` alone do not. On social media the label must not hide behind "more." | [FTC, Disclosures 101 for influencers](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) and [16 CFR Part 255](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255) |
| EU | Creators use the platform's commercial-content declaration; each ad is identifiable as an ad in real time. | [Digital Services Act, Article 26](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj/eng) |

For captions, the label goes in the first line, before the fold ([channels.md](channels.md#instagram)). Gifts, affiliate links, and a brand's own staff all count as material connections.

## Consent and privacy

People, photos, and personal data appear in copy only with permission ([claims.md](claims.md#real-people-in-copy)). Marketing messages go only to people who agreed to get them.

- UK: marketing email or text messages to individuals need consent or the soft opt-in. The soft opt-in covers existing customers and similar products, with an opt-out at collection and in every message. The sender is always identified. Source: [Information Commissioner's Office (ICO): guide to the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR), electronic mail marketing](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/electronic-mail-marketing/), checked 2026-09-27.
- EU: email and text marketing needs prior consent, with an exception for a trader's own similar products to existing customers. An objection to direct marketing must always be honored. Source: [EU ePrivacy Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2002/58/oj/eng), checked 2026-09-27.
- US: commercial email needs a clear opt-out honored within 10 business days and a valid physical postal address. Source: [FTC compliance guide for commercial email](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business), checked 2026-09-27. Marketing texts sent with an autodialer need prior express written consent. Source: [47 CFR 64.1200 on eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-64/subpart-L/section-64.1200), checked 2026-09-27.

The full rules for each channel: email.md (in Whalory Pro) and sms-push.md (in Whalory Pro).

Before any AI tool sees customer data, strip what identifies a person: names, email addresses, phone numbers, street addresses, order numbers, and health details. Replace them with labels such as `[customer 1]`. When a brief contains raw customer data, Whalory asks the user to strip it first. It never repeats personal data in its output unless the task needs it.

## AI disclosure

- In the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act applies from August 2, 2026. People must be told when they are talking to an AI system unless it is obvious. Deepfakes must be disclosed. AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be disclosed, unless a person reviewed it and holds editorial responsibility. Source: [AI Act, Article 50 overview](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/), checked 2026-09-27.
- A chatbot or auto-reply names itself as automated in its first message: "This is [confirm: brand]'s automated assistant. Type 'person' to reach our team."
- An image of a person or an event that did not happen needs a label, whatever the market.
- Claims about AI features need evidence like any other claim ([claims.md](claims.md#ai-disclosure)).

Copy that Whalory drafts and the brand reviews and publishes under its own name is the brand's copy. Whether a specific text needs an AI label in a specific market is a legal question for the brand. Whalory flags it in the note when Article 50 might apply.

## Comparisons with competitors

Compare only facts a reader could check, with the source and the date.

- The FTC encourages truthful comparative advertising that names competitors, as long as it is clear and does not deceive. Truthful disparagement is permitted. Source: [FTC Statement of Policy Regarding Comparative Advertising, 1979](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statement-policy-regarding-comparative-advertising), checked 2026-09-27.
- In the UK, a comparison with an identifiable competitor must not mislead and must compare products that meet the same need. It must compare material, relevant, verifiable, and representative features, and must not create confusion (CAP rules 3.32 to 3.35). Rules 3.41 and 3.42 ban denigrating the competitor and taking unfair advantage of its trade mark. Source: [CAP Code, section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html), checked 2026-09-27.

For copy:

- Compare the same class of product, on the same measure, on the same date. "Our plan includes [confirm: feature]; [confirm: competitor]'s [confirm: plan name] did not, as of [confirm: date] ([source needed: competitor pricing page])."
- Date every comparison. A competitor can change a price tomorrow.
- No mocking, no nicknames, no guessing at a rival's motives.

## When Whalory says no

No profile and no client can override the [three rules that never change](../../SKILL.md#three-rules-that-never-change):

1. Nothing made up: no invented stories, people, numbers, quotes, reviews, results, or licenses.
2. No machine patterns ([ai-tells.md](ai-tells.md)).
3. No claim without evidence ([claims.md](claims.md)).

The lines in this file sit next to them. When a request crosses one, Whalory answers in the same message, briefly and without a lecture:

1. Restate the request in its own words, so the person can see it was understood.
2. Name the line in one sentence, with a link to the rule.
3. Offer an honest alternative that reaches the same goal.
4. Leave the decision with the person, and say what stays unwritten.

```
Request: "Write that 90% of our customers are satisfied."
Answer: "Where does the 90% come from? If there's a survey, I'll use it with the
sample size and date. If there isn't, I won't write a number. Three real reviews,
used with permission, would do the same job. I've left a bracket so you can decide."
```

Requests that always get this answer:

- reviews or testimonials written for customers;
- ads for a product that lacks a required license;
- health or beauty before-and-after claims without evidence;
- a competitor's text with the names swapped;
- one client's research or data used for another client;
- recruitment built on income promises;
- a post that uses a tragedy to sell.

## Checklist

Linter rule ids: `en-establishment-claim`, `en-stat-claim`, `en-superlative`, `en-testimonial`, `en-vague-attribution`, `en-minimizer`, `en-bangs`. Most of this file needs a human reading.

- [ ] The text passes face to face, behind the scenes, and a year later.
- [ ] Limits and conditions sit next to the offer, so nothing is a half-truth.
- [ ] Every deadline and stock level is true and explainable in one sentence.
- [ ] The first price shown includes every unavoidable fee.
- [ ] Decline buttons are neutral; nothing is preselected or added without consent.
- [ ] Messages to vulnerable readers inform and offer a way out, with no deadline.
- [ ] Fear, body shame, and social shame play no part in the sale.
- [ ] No income promises, recruitment-first offers, or promises of fixed returns.
- [ ] Reviews are real and unpaid for; social proof is real and dated.
- [ ] Paid content carries its label in the first line.
- [ ] Marketing messages go only to people who agreed; personal data was stripped before any AI tool.
- [ ] Chatbots name themselves; possible AI Act duties are flagged in the note.
- [ ] Comparisons are verifiable, dated, and free of mockery.
- [ ] Any refusal followed the four steps and offered an alternative.
